# Pediatric Self-Hypnosis and Bedwetting: What Published Research Can—and Cannot—Tell Us

## What is pediatric self-hypnosis?

Pediatric self-hypnosis is a learnable attention skill. A child focuses on a calming idea, image, or bodily feeling while listening to carefully chosen suggestions. The child remains aware and involved; the aim is not to surrender control, but to practice using attention and imagination in a purposeful way. In the pediatric literature, hypnosis is described as a way to build self-regulation: the ability to shift attention, manage reactions, and rehearse a preferred response.[1]

For children, this work is usually playful and collaborative. A clinician may invite a child to imagine a favorite place, notice comfortable breathing, or picture a personal “control panel.” The language is adapted to the child’s age, interests, and own description of the problem. That individualized, choice-based quality is central to the approach developed in pediatric hypnosis—not a rigid script delivered in the same way to everyone.[1]

## Who are Dr. Karen Olness and Dr. Daniel Kohen?

Dr. Karen Olness is a pediatrician and Professor Emerita of Pediatrics, Global Health and Diseases at Case Western Reserve University. The National Pediatric Hypnosis Training Institute describes her as board certified in Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics and notes her past leadership of several professional hypnosis and pediatric organizations.[3] She is widely identified in the field’s historical literature as one of the early pioneers of modern pediatric clinical hypnosis.[1]

Dr. Daniel P. Kohen is a retired developmental-behavioral pediatrician and Professor Emeritus of Pediatrics and Family Medicine and Community Health at the University of Minnesota. He was a co-founder and education leader of the National Pediatric Hypnosis Training Institute, and his professional work has included clinical practice, research, teaching, and clinician education.[3]

Their collaboration is especially important because it helped organize pediatric hypnosis as a child-centered, skills-based field. The first edition of *Hypnosis and Hypnotherapy with Children* was published in 1981 by G. Gail Gardner and Karen Olness. Olness and Kohen later co-authored the third edition in 1996 and the fourth edition in 2011. The fourth edition brought together historical background, clinical guidance, case material, and research concerning hypnosis with children and adolescents.[1][2]

## How the general method works

The method can be understood as a sequence of connected elements rather than a single formula. First comes **rapport**: the adult listens, explains what will happen in plain language, learns what matters to the child, and notices existing strengths. Motivation and a sense of choice matter. The child is presented as an active participant who can accept, change, or stop an exercise.[1]

Next comes **induction**, meaning the transition into a calm, focused state. With children, this may involve favorite-place imagery, breathing, progressive relaxation, a story, drawing, or another activity that naturally absorbs attention. Relaxation can be helpful, but it is not the only defining feature. The key is focused attention and imaginative involvement.[1]

The central suggestions are then connected to the child’s goals. They are often multisensory and open-ended: the child might imagine what safety, steadiness, confidence, or successful communication feels like. The clinician uses the child’s own words and interests instead of imposing an unfamiliar picture. This can make the exercise easier to remember and practice independently.[1]

Finally comes **reinforcement**. The child practices the skill between sessions, sometimes using a short bedtime routine or a personal cue. Follow-up gives the clinician and family an opportunity to review what felt useful, adjust the imagery, and encourage continued practice without shame or pressure. The word “reinforcement” here means strengthening a learned self-regulation skill, not blaming a child when progress is uneven.[1][5]

## How the approach has been applied to nighttime wetting

The published literature does not establish one universally validated, fixed three-phase protocol for every child. However, its recurring elements can be explained in three practical phases: relationship-building and preparation; focused imagery with goal-directed suggestions; and reinforcement through practice and follow-up.[1][5]

In an early report, Karen Olness described teaching self-hypnosis to 40 children with childhood nocturnal enuresis, the clinical term used for nighttime wetting.[4] PubMed confirms the report’s author, patient group, journal, year, and DOI, although no abstract is available in the database. Because the full report is not freely summarized there, it is best treated as an early clinical report—not as proof that the method works for every child.[4]

A later pediatric hypnosis review illustrates how bladder–brain imagery can be personalized. In the example, a child who was dry during the day learned to picture the bladder as a container, the brain as the body’s “main computer,” and the communication between them as a two-way message. The bedtime suggestion was to rehearse a calm plan: if the bladder became full, the brain could signal waking and a trip to the bathroom; alternatively, the bladder could remain closed until morning. The point of the image was to replace a frightening or shame-filled story with a cooperative mind–body pattern that the child could rehearse.[1]

A prospective 2013 practice-based study followed 17 children who received hypnosis for nocturnal enuresis. The authors reported that 11 children had an initial parent rating in the good-to-excellent range and 10 had a good-to-excellent long-term rating. The average follow-up was about 25 months for the full 53-child study, and no parent or child reported an adverse effect. The investigators also emphasized important limitations: the sample was small, there was no control group, the parent rating scale was not validated, and home practice and return visits were inconsistent.[5]

More recently, a 2024 prospective single-center pilot study enrolled 17 children aged 8 to 15 with primary monosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis. Twelve recorded at least one month of post-program data. The median number of dry nights rose from 8 during the baseline month to 15.5 by the third month after a self-guided online hypnosis program; the reported statistical comparison was *P* = .0033, and all children who completed the study showed an improvement.[6] These findings are encouraging but preliminary. The study was small, did not randomly compare hypnosis with another approach, and was connected to one commercially available program. It therefore cannot establish how well the same approach would work for all children or families.[6]
